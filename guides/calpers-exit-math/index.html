CalPERS Exit Math.
When can you leave, and what is it worth?

By Stephen Dugger, a CalPERS safety member.

I started running these numbers for myself. First I wanted to see what my pension would actually be when I retire, and what that check is worth in today's dollars, so I could put it in context. Then came the real questions. What's the most economical date for me to leave? How long do I gut it out? Where's the efficient frontier, the point where one more year stops paying for itself?

Most of us know our pension formula. Fewer of us have run the numbers on leaving: what changes if you go at 50 instead of 52, what you lose if you walk at 49, and why one month can be worth thousands of dollars a year for the rest of your life.

This guide is the math. Five numbers decide your leave date. One formula turns them into a monthly check. A short list of mistakes costs people real money, and most of them can't be undone. At the end there's a checklist of what to pull and where to verify it.

Everything here comes from CalPERS's own pages and the Government Code, cited as we go. Your case may differ. Check yours.

The five numbers that decide your leave date

1. Service credit. Your years of CalPERS service, partial years included. CalPERS counts it on a fiscal-year basis, July 1 to June 30. You need at least five years to retire for service, with some exceptions. [1]

2. Age factor. The percentage of pay you earn for each year of service. It depends on your formula and your age on the day you retire, not the day you were hired. Under the PEPRA local safety formula 2.7% at 57, the factor is 2.0% at 50 and climbs 0.1% a year to 2.7% at 57. It moves every quarter year of age (2.025% at 50¼, 2.050% at 50½). Minimum retirement age is 50. After 57 it stops rising. [2]

3. Final compensation. For PEPRA members, your highest average pensionable pay over at least 36 consecutive months. [3] Not everything on your check counts, so ask HR which pay items are pensionable. PEPRA also caps the pay that counts. For 2026 the cap is $191,679 if your agency doesn't pay into Social Security and $159,733 if it does. [4] Most officers are well under it.

4. The retiree-health vesting schedule. If your agency has adopted Government Code 22893, the employer's share of your retiree health premium depends on your years of credited service at retirement: nothing under 10 years, 50% at 10, plus 5% for each additional year, 100% at 20. At least five of those years must be with the agency you retire from. [5] The percentage applies to a base amount set by formula each year, not to whatever plan you choose. Here's the catch: 22893 only applies where the agency elected it by resolution and, for represented employees, agreed to it with the bargaining unit. [5] Many agencies use a flat dollar amount or a different schedule instead. Your MOU is where you find out which one you have.

5. The 120-day rule. To keep CalPERS health in retirement, your retirement date must be within 120 days of your separation date, and you must retire from an agency that contracts with CalPERS for health for your employee group. More than 120 days apart, and your eligibility "cannot be reinstated." [6] This is the rule that turns a timing mistake into a permanent one.

The formula, in plain words

Years of service × age factor × final monthly compensation = your monthly pension (the "unmodified allowance," which pays the most and leaves no survivor benefit). [1]

That's it. The hard part is that two of the three inputs depend on when you leave, and the health share rides on top.

A worked example (hypothetical)

Meet Sam. Sam is made up. Sam was hired at 36 into a CalPERS agency on the PEPRA 2.7% at 57 formula. The agency has adopted GC 22893. Sam's final compensation is $9,000 a month ($108,000 a year). To keep the comparison clean I hold that pay flat in today's dollars. Real raises would make the later dates worth more, not less.

Read the table two ways. Going down, each extra year adds service and raises the factor at the same time, so the pension grows faster than the calendar. From 50 to 57 Sam's check roughly doubles. Going across, the health share is its own benefit. If the base amount were around $1,000 a month (a round number for illustration, not a quote), 70% is about $700 a month on top of the pension, every month, adjusted as premiums rise.

Classic members: different formula, same method

If you were a CalPERS member before January 1, 2013, you may be a classic member on a formula like 3% at 50. That one works differently. The factor is 3.0% at 50 and doesn't rise after that, and the maximum is 90% of pay. [7] Your final compensation period may be 12 months instead of 36, depending on your agency's contract. [1] There are also PEPRA safety formulas other than 2.7% at 57 (2% at 57 and 2.5% at 57). [3] The method above works for all of them. The numbers don't. Look up your own formula on your Annual Member Statement and use your own chart. [8]

The expensive mistakes

Leaving one month before a step. The health schedule moves on completed years. If Sam left at 50 with 13 years and 11 months, the share would be 65%, not 70%. On a $1,000 base that's $50 a month, for life, for one month of work. Now take a deputy hired at 41 who leaves at 50 with 9 years and 11 months. The share is zero. One more month and it's 50%. Smaller steps exist too: the age factor rises every quarter year of age, and longevity or step raises can lift your final compensation if they land inside your highest 36 months. Before you pick a date, list every step that lands in the following 12 months.

Separating without retiring inside 120 days. People do this by accident. They leave for a private job, plan to "deal with the pension later," and find out at 57 that CalPERS health is gone for good. [6] Two things make this worse for safety members. First, you can't retire before 50 for service, so if you separate before 50 you can't retire within 120 days. Unless you land at another CalPERS employer and retire from there, separating before 50 means no CalPERS retiree health. [1][6] Second, the 120 days also governs unused sick leave. Where your agency contracts for it, sick leave converts to service credit (2,000 hours = one year) only if you retire within 120 days of separating. [9] And don't count on sick leave to carry you across a health step. It adds service credit to your pension, but GC 22893 counts only service you were paid for while you did it, so sick-leave credit very likely doesn't add a year to your health share. [5][15] If your date depends on it, ask CalPERS in writing.

Deferring when retiring now pays more. A deferred pension uses the age factor at the age you start it, but your final compensation stays frozen at what you earned when you left. Look at Sam at 50. Retire now: $2,520 a month starting at 50. Defer to 57: $3,402 a month starting at 57. Retiring now collects about $211,680 before 57. Deferring pays $882 a month more after that. With no cost-of-living raises, deferring catches up at 77. But CalPERS pays its COLA (2% a year on most local safety contracts) starting the second May after you retire, so the check Sam starts at 50 has been rising for years by the time the deferred one begins. Counting a 2% COLA on both, deferring doesn't catch up until about 85, and that's before counting the health share deferring gives up. [14] Once you're past 50 and past the 10-year health mark, deferring rarely wins. Run your own numbers before you assume it does.

Taking a refund. When you leave you can leave your contributions on deposit and retire later, or take a refund. A refund returns your contributions and interest, not the employer's money. It ends your membership and forfeits future benefits. [10][11] You can sometimes buy it back if you return to CalPERS work, but it costs more than you took out and the price grows with interest. [11]

Moving systems without checking reciprocity. Many counties run their own retirement systems under the County Employees Retirement Law of 1937 (the "'37 Act" systems). Many of those, plus some city systems, are reciprocal with CalPERS. [12] Reciprocity can let the highest final compensation from either system count toward both pensions. [13] The conditions are strict. You start in the new system within six months of leaving the old one. You can't overlap, and burning leave counts as still employed. You leave your CalPERS money on deposit. You generally retire from both systems on the same date. [12][13] One more trap: a classic member who moves to a different CalPERS employer after a break of more than six months comes back as a PEPRA member. [3] If a lateral is anywhere in your future, treat this section as "check your own case," and do it before you give notice.

The one-page checklist

From myCalPERS (my.calpers.ca.gov):

  • ☐ Your Annual Member Statement: formula, membership date, classic or PEPRA, service credit to date. [8]
  • ☐ Retirement estimates at three or four dates: the earliest you'd leave, your 10-year health mark, a date or two after, and 57. Save each one. [8]
  • ☐ Whether each estimate assumes today's pay or projected pay. They read very differently.
  • ☐ Your contribution balance (what a refund would pay, and what you'd give up).

From your MOU:

  • ☐ The retiree-health section. Does it cite GC 22893, a flat dollar amount, or something else? Which hire dates does it cover?
  • ☐ Which pay items are pensionable (POST pay, longevity, special assignments).
  • ☐ Sick leave conversion: does your agency contract for it?
  • ☐ When the current MOU expires. Retiree-health terms are bargained.

From HR:

  • ☐ Your exact separation date mechanics, and how leave payouts affect it.
  • ☐ Your credited service as of each date you're considering, and whether any purchased or prior service counts toward the health schedule.
  • ☐ Whether your agency pays into Social Security (it changes your PEPRA cap). [4]
  • ☐ If a lateral is possible: the new agency's retirement system and whether it's reciprocal. [12]

Your dates, written down:

  • ☐ Age 50 date. 10-year credited service date. Each later service anniversary. 57.
  • ☐ For your chosen date: separation date, retirement date, and the gap between them (it must be 120 days or less to keep health). [6]

Where to verify

To run your own dates first, the free CalPERS Leave Date tool does this math on your numbers and lists the steps in the twelve months after any date you pick. Then check it. CalPERS is the authority on your pension, and your MOU and HR are the authority on your retiree health. This guide is neither. Call 888 CalPERS (888-225-7377), book a retirement planning appointment, and run the myCalPERS estimator yourself.
